Every SaaS product begins with the same six weeks. Sign-up and sign-in. Email
verification that survives a user clicking the link twice. Sessions that can be
revoked from another device. An invitation flow. Roles. An organization that
owns things, and a query layer that will not let one organization read another's
rows. None of it is what the product is for, and all of it has to be right.

The work is not hard, exactly. It is *detailed*. The interesting failures are
the ones that only appear under load, or on the second attempt, or when a
webhook arrives twice because a network hiccuped somewhere upstream. Those are
the parts a team writes late, under pressure, after the demo has already been
promised.

## What we decided to do instead

Write it once, carefully, with tests, and treat the result as the floor rather
than the product. Everything in this template exists because leaving it out
meant someone would rebuild it three weeks later with less time to think.

That has one uncomfortable consequence, which is worth saying out loud: a
template accumulates opinions. Ours are written down as architecture decision
records rather than left implicit in the code, so the ones you disagree with are
at least easy to find and argue with.

## What it is not

It is not a framework. There is no plugin API to learn and nothing that will
fight you when you delete a directory. The modules are switched off in a config
file and take their routes, their tables and their interface with them — which
is a design constraint the whole codebase is held to, not a feature bolted on
afterwards.

If your product turns out not to need billing, the honest answer is to turn
billing off and stop paying for its existence.
